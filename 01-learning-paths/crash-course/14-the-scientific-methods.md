# Crash Course History of Science #14: The Scientific Methods

## Source

## Topics Covered

- The idea that there is no single timeless "scientific method."
- Galileo's role as an experimental and mathematical natural philosopher.
- The telescope as an example of instruments reshaping scientific practice.
- Francis Bacon's practical, experimental, organized, and socially useful model of knowledge-making.
- Rene Descartes's methodological doubt, rationalism, and mathematical style of reasoning.
- Induction, deduction, experimentation, rationality, and self-examination as emerging norms.
- The relationship between scientific method, state power, war, and colonial expansion.

## Summary

Episode 14 reframes "the scientific method" as a historical development rather than a fixed recipe. Instead of presenting one universal sequence, it compares several early modern approaches to reliable knowledge.

Galileo represents mathematical experimentation, careful comparison of theories, and the way instruments such as the telescope changed scientific practice. Bacon represents a practical and collective vision of inquiry: observation, experiment, useful knowledge, and organized research. Descartes represents radical doubt, rational order, deduction, and the search for certainty.

Together, these figures help explain why early modern natural philosophy increasingly valued testing, reasoned argument, public evidence, repeatability, and disciplined skepticism. The episode also keeps an important complication in view: methods of science were tied not only to truth-seeking but also to states, warfare, navigation, commerce, and empire.

## Important Dates

- 1561: Francis Bacon is born.
- 1564: Galileo Galilei is born in Pisa.
- 1592: Galileo begins teaching at the University of Padua.
- 1593: Galileo works as a ballistics consultant at the Venetian Arsenal.
- 1596: Rene Descartes is born.
- 1608: Hans Lippershey seeks a telescope patent in the Dutch Republic.
- 1609: Galileo builds and refines telescopes for astronomical use.
- 1620: Bacon publishes `Novum Organum`.
- 1632: Galileo publishes `Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems`.
- 1633: Descartes suppresses publication of `Le Monde` after Galileo's trial.
- 1637: Descartes publishes `Discourse on Method`.

## Questions Raised

- Why do we talk about "the scientific method" when historically there were many scientific methods?
- What changes when instruments become central to observation?
- How are induction and deduction different as ways of making knowledge?
- What social rules make experiments trustworthy to other people?
- How did early modern states, war, navigation, and colonial projects shape scientific practice?
- How should the repository represent methods: as concepts, practices, institutions, or all three?
