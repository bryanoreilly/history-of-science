# Crash Course History of Science #13: The New Astronomy

## Source

- Official page: https://thecrashcourse.com/courses/the-new-astronomy-crash-course-history-of-science-13/
- YouTube video: https://youtube.com/watch?v=-FYvy3_egHw
- Transcript reference: https://nerdfighteria.info/v/-FYvy3_egHw/

## Topics Covered

- The transition from Copernican heliocentrism to early modern mathematical astronomy.
- Tycho Brahe's observatories, instruments, and high-precision naked-eye observations.
- The Tychonic geo-heliocentric model.
- The 1572 "new star" and the weakening of the idea of unchanging heavens.
- Johannes Kepler's use of Tycho's data, especially the orbit of Mars.
- Kepler's first two laws of planetary motion in `Astronomia nova` and the later third law in `Harmonice Mundi`.
- Galileo's telescopic observations of the Moon and Jupiter.
- Galileo's public defense of Copernican astronomy and the limits of his tidal argument.

## Summary

Episode 13 follows the "new astronomy" from Copernicus toward Galileo by focusing on two crucial figures between them: Tycho Brahe and Johannes Kepler. Tycho created a major observational program on Hven, built Uraniborg and Stjerneborg, and produced exceptionally precise pre-telescopic data. Kepler then used Tycho's observations, especially of Mars, to move beyond uniform circular motion and formulate elliptical planetary motion.

The episode closes by turning to Galileo. His telescope-supported observations in `Sidereus Nuncius` challenged inherited Aristotelian and Ptolemaic assumptions by showing an uneven Moon and bodies orbiting Jupiter. Galileo's later `Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems` widened the public debate over evidence, cosmology, and scientific method, even though his argument from tides was incorrect.

This episode is a hinge: astronomy becomes a field where long-term observation, mathematical modeling, instrumentation, publication, patronage, and religious politics all interact.

## Important Dates

- 1543: Copernicus's `De revolutionibus orbium coelestium` is published.
- 1546: Tycho Brahe is born.
- 1564: Galileo Galilei is born.
- 1566: Tycho loses part of his nose in a duel.
- 1571: Johannes Kepler is born.
- 1572: Tycho observes the new star in Cassiopeia.
- 1573: Tycho publishes `De nova stella`.
- 1600: Kepler moves to Prague to work with Tycho.
- 1601: Tycho dies; Kepler succeeds him as Imperial Mathematician.
- 1604: Kepler observes a new star.
- 1609: Kepler publishes `Astronomia nova`; Galileo begins using and improving telescopes for astronomical work.
- 1610: Galileo publishes `Sidereus Nuncius`.
- 1619: Kepler publishes the third law in `Harmonice Mundi`.
- 1632: Galileo publishes `Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems`.
- 1638: Galileo publishes `Two New Sciences`.

## Questions Raised

- How did Tycho's observational program make Kepler's mathematical astronomy possible?
- Why were circular orbits and uniform motion so durable as assumptions?
- What counted as good evidence in early modern astronomy: observation, mathematical fit, physical explanation, scripture, tradition, or public persuasion?
- How did patronage and court politics shape astronomical research?
- Why did Galileo reject or ignore some of Kepler's ideas while defending Copernicanism?
- What did telescopes change about the relationship between instruments and evidence?
